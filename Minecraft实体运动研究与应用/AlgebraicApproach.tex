\chapter{Forewords}

As an important part of technological Minecraft, motion of entities has always been one of central topics in the community, and countless well-known applications (e.g. pearl cannons, border traveling and item transportation) have resulted. However, there are rarely dedicated discussion on specific mechanisms for the motion itself, compared to the hottest topics like block updates and mob spawning which has been thoroughly explained, experimented and applied by numerous researchers and engineers.

Sets of kinematic formulas for specific types of entities of interest like TNTs and terminologies like momentum were put into usage as early as middle 2010s and field experts like cannon designers had their own empirical and mathematical models on the topics they concerned at that time.

However, a unified framework isn't formulated until July 2021 when lovexyn0827 released their 70k words dissertation\cite{2021}, the first complete discussion on our topic ever publicly available, which extensively covers from fundamental theoretical grounding and complete kinematic formulas different update orders, to detailed analysis on a range of mechanisms and almost every negligible corner cases ever practically related. Despite its fundamental contribution to theorists, it is commonly considered "too hard to begin with" due to its complex monolithic theoretical framework, and its sheer tediousity is enough to scare readers off. Nevertheless, it marks a mew era of researches on motion of entities.

A newer book \cite{MCSE} releases a few weeks ago by MCS\&E has adopted an axiomized approach to present mechanisms in a rigorous while pedagogically viable way. Asides from inheriting lovexyn0827's theoretical framework with radical optimization, it has employed its on educational strategies and introduced field analysis to its analytical toolkit to gain new a new insight by visualization and calculation. Well-structured and self-contained though, it lakes proper treatment to many topics like dimensionalities (a.k.a. units), collision detection, floating point errors and piston push, which is essential in engineering practices. Besides, it has ignored the discrete nature of Minecraft before diving to continuous approximations too deep, inspect of which its asymptotic analysis has contributed much to practices.

Therefore, we are writing the book to suggest a complete and mathematical rigorous framework applicable to both continuous approximation and machinery reality. Our approach to kinematics is:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item To devise a unified framework compatible with any algebraic structure with arithmetics;
	\item Study two special cases,
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item One for real numbers, to calculate and approximate;
		\item Another for floating point numbers, to bound errors;
	\end{enumerate}
	\item Combine both to establish a approximation along with a (hard or probabilistic) bound, for "physically" rigorous analysis.
\end{enumerate}

Furthermore, a universal model for world and ticking is established, and will lead us to a novel paradigm of game mechanism analysis.

The book will be separated to seven chapters, asides from the appendix:

\section{Logic}

We are going to reintroduce basic grammars of mathematical logic informally, and we shall continue with this fashion in sections to come (for this chapter). For a more formal introduction with complete technological details, see \cite{GTM53-Ch1}. Most definitions is this chapter are not indexed to keep conciseness.

Logical predicates are statements that is either true or false, that is, to have truth value $\true$ or $\false$. Logical predicates are closed if they have definite truth values.

We shall adopt conventional logical operators. Let $p$, $q$ be logical predicates, $p\land q = \true$ iff both $p$ and $q$ is $\true$, $p\lor q = \true$ iff at least one of $p$ and $q$ is true, and $\neg p = \true$ iff $p = \false$, $p\implies q = \neg p \lor q$, $p \iff q = p\implies q \land q\land p$. We shall adopt conventional operator precedence, that is,  $\neg$ evaluates before $\land$, $\lor$, $\implies$ and $\iff$.

For a given logical predicate $p$, $\forall xp$ is true iff $p = \true$ for all $x$'s; $\exists xp$ is true iff there is at least one $x$ such that $p$ is true.

\section{Set Theory}

We shall adopt Zermelo-Fraenkel axioms for set theories with the Axiom of Choice, and the convention that all mathematical objects are exactly all sets shall be conformed.

As a informal restatement, ZFC axioms specifies sets as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item There is an empty set $\emptyset$ such that $\forall x(x\notin \emptyset)$.
	\item Two sets (mathematical objects) are equal iff they contain exactly the same members: $a = b \iff (\forall x(x\in a \iff x\in b))$.
	\item Given any pair of mathematical objects, say $x$ and $y$, there is a set $\{x, y\}$ containing exactly such objects.
	\item Given a set $S$ of sets, there is a set $\cup S$ containing exactly all objects that is contained by some members of $S$: $\forall (x \in \cup S \iff \exists y (y\in S \land x\in y))$.
	\item Given any set $S$, there is a set $2^S = \{x: x\subset S\}$ of all subsets of $S$.
	\item Given a set $S$ and a logical predicate $p$, there is a set $\{x: x\in S \land p(x)\}$.
	\item Any non-empty set $S$ has a member $x$ disjoint with $S$, that is, $x\cap S = \emptyset$, for regularity.
	\item There is a set $y$ of countably infinite members: $\exists y(\emptyset \in y \land (x\in y \implies \{x\} \in y))$
	\item Given a set of disjoint nonempty sets, there is a set containing exactly one member in each disjoint set.
\end{enumerate}

Note that there are collections of objects which is not sets, which is called $proper classes$, see Exercise \ref{exe:sets-or-pc}. Strictly speaking, proper classes are not valid mathematical objects. It worth a restatement that mathematical objects are exactly sets, and the two terms can thus be used interchangeably, only with intuitive difference that mathematical objects are arbitrary things (perhaps conceptually atomic points) and sets are collections of other elements.

Set operations, i.e. $\cup$, $\cap$, $-$ (minus), $\bigcup_{i \in I}$, , $\bigcap_{i \in I}$, are defined as in the convention.

Given two mathematical objects $x$ and $y$, $\left<x, y\right> = \{\{x\}, \{x, y\}\}$ are ordered pairs with left component $x$ and right component $y$. Given two sets $A$ and $B$, the Cartesian product of them is the denoted $A\times B = \{\left<a, b\right>: a\in A\land b\in B\}$. Subsets of $A\times B$ are binary relations on $A$ and $B$.

Given a binary relation on $A$ and $B$, if $\left<x, y\right>\in R$, $x$ and $y$ are said to conform $R$, which is denoted $xRy$.

Function are binary relations such that left components are different for different ordered pairs contained, that is, for all functions $f$, $xfy \land zfw \implies x = z$. The set of all functions from $A$ to $B$ are denoted $B^A = \{f: f\subset A\times B \land(xfy \land zfw\implies x = z)\}$. 

Given a function $f \in B^A$ and $x\in A$, the image of $x$ under $f$ is theunique $y$ such that $xfy$, namely $f(x)$. Function $f$ are denoted $x\mapsto f(x)(x\in A)$ (e.g. $x\to x^2(x\in\mathbb R)$, where $(x\in \mathbb R)$ etc. can be omitted if clear from the context) if the name is irrelevant or $f: A\to B$ if the domain $A$ and (an enclosure of) range $B$ (instead of specific mapping rules) are to emphasis. Likewise, for any $X\subset A$, $f(X) = \{y:y = f(x)\land x\in S\}$ is the image of $S$ under $f$. The domain $A$ is denoted $\operatorname{dom} f$, and the range of $f$ is $\operatorname{im} f = f(A) \subset B$. If $A'\subset A$, $f_{A'} = \left\{\left<x, y\right>: x\in A'\land y = f(x)\right\}$ is called $f$ restricted to $A'$.

Natural examples of functions are:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item $I_A: x\mapsto x~(x\in A)$
	\item $\mathbb 1_{A, B}: x\mapsto \begin{cases}
		1 & x\in A\\
		0 & x\in B - A
	\end{cases}$, where $B$ can be omitted when irrelevant
\end{enumerate}

A function $f: A\to B$ is surjective if $f(A) = B$, is injective if $f(a)  = f(b) \iff a = b$ for all $a, b \in A$, and is bijective if it is both surjective and injective.

Given functions $f: A\to B$ and $g: B\to C$, $f\circ g = \{\left<x, y\right>: \exists x\exists y\exists z(y = f(x) \land z = f(y))\}$ denotes the function such that for all $x\in A$, $(f\circ g)(x) = g(f(x))$. We are adopting such a left composition notation so that the order in which operators applies is consistent with the left-to-right lexual order in witch they are printed. For repeated composition and inversion, notations like $f^{n}$, $f^{-n}$, etc. are defined as conventionally expected.

Two sets $A$ and $B$ are equipotent iff there is a bijective function $f: A\to B$, that is, especially intuitive for finite sets, two sets have the same number of members. Equipotent sets share the same cardinal, and for a set $A$, $|A|$ is its (unique) cardinal. Cardinals can be thought of as an "extension" of natural numbers: for finite sets, $|A|$ is the number of (distinct) members in $A$; and for infinite sets, more subtle notations are uses for cardinals, such as $\aleph_0$ for the cardinal of the set of all integers and possibly $\aleph_1$ for all real numbers. A set $A$ is countable iff $|A|\le \aleph_0$, or finite if $|A|<\aleph_0$, where the meaning of comparison is obvious for qualified theorists. 

A equivalence relation on set $S$ is a subset $E$ of $S\times S$ such that

\begin{enumerate}
	\item $\forall x(x\in S\implies \left<x, x\right>\in E)$
	\item $\forall x\forall y(\left<x, y\right>\in E\implies \left<y, x\right>\in E)$
	\item $\forall x\forall y \forall z(\left<x, y\right>\in E\land\left<y, z\right>\in E\implies \left<x, z\right>\in E)$
\end{enumerate}

For any $x\in A$, $\overline{x} = \{y: xSy\} = \{y: ySx\}$ is the equivalence class containing $x$. A partition of set $A$ is a set of disjoint sets $\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ (indexed by set $I$), such that $A_i\cap A_j \ne \emptyset \iff i = j$ and $\cup(\{A_i\}_{i\in I}) = A$. The set $A/S = \{\overline{x}: x\in A\}$ is a partition of $A$ is called the equivalence classes in $A$, or the quotient set of $A$ and $S$.

A partial order relation on set $S$ is a subset $P$ of $S\times S$ such that

\begin{enumerate}
	\item $\forall x(x\in S\implies \left<x, x\right>\in P)$
	\item $\forall x\forall y(\left<x, y\right>\in P\implies (\left<y, x\right>\in P\iff x = y))$
	\item $\forall x\forall y \forall z(\left<x, y\right>\in P\land\left<y, z\right>\in P\implies \left<x, z\right>\in P)$
\end{enumerate}

A set $S$ is partially ordered under $P$ if $P$ is a partial order relation on $S$, and then pair $\left<S, P\right>$ is a partially ordered set; in particular, if $P$ is a linear order relation, then $S$ is linearly ordered under $P$ and $\left<S, P\right>$ is a linearly ordered set. 

Given two partially ordered sets $\left<S, P\right>$ and $\left<S', P'\right>$, a isomorphism is a bijective function $f: S\to S'$ such that

\begin{equation*}
	xPy \iff f(x)P'f(y)
\end{equation*}

$\left<S, P\right>$ and $\left<S', P'\right>$ are isomorphic (written $\left<S, P\right> \cong \left<S', P'\right>$) if such isomorphism exists.

Followings are the sets which are used throughout the book:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item $\mathbb N = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ of all natural numbers. Technically, one may have $0 = \emptyset$, and $x + 1 = x\cup\{x\}$ for $x\ge 0$. $\overline{\mathbb N} =\mathbb Q\cup\{+\infty\}$ and $\mathbb N_+ = \mathbb N - \{0\}$ is defined for convenience.
	\item $\mathbb Z$ of all integers, $\overline{\mathbb Z} =\mathbb Z\cup\{-\infty, +\infty\}$, and $\mathbb Z_n$ for integer $i$'s such that $0\le i< n$.
	\item $\mathbb Q$ of all rationals numbers, $\overline{\mathbb Q} =\mathbb Q\cup\{+\infty\}$.
	\item $\mathbb R$ of all real numbers, $\overline{\mathbb R} =\mathbb R\cup\{-\infty, +\infty\}$.
\end{enumerate}

Ordering and arithmetic on numeric sets are defined naturally; for undefined arithmetical operations such as $+\infty - (+\infty)$ and $+\infty\times 0$, we simply define them as 0 for definitity since we are not going to handle such expressions at all.

\begin{definition}
	The product of a set $\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ of sets indexed by $I$ is the set of all function $f$'s from $I$ to $\cup\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ such that $f(i)\in A_i$, denoted $\prod_{i\in I} A_i$.

	If $I = \mathbb Z_k$, alternative notation $\prod_{i=0}^{k - 1} A_k = \prod_{i\in Z_k} A_i$ is used, likewise $\prod_{i = 0}^{\infty} = \prod_{i\in\mathbb N} A_i$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a finite set $\{A_i\}_{i\in \mathbb Z_k}$ of sets, the canonical representation of $A_0\times \cdots \times A_{k - 1}$ is $\prod_{i\in \mathbb Z_k} A_i$, which is well defined despite the potential ambiguity of association between operator $\times$'s.
\end{definition}

We shall abuse the notation slightly to pretend that $\left<x_0, x_1\right> = i\mapsto x_i$ in following chapters, since the previous definition that $\left<x, y\right> = \{\{x\}, \{x, y\}\}$ is now useless after defining functions. 

For canonical representations of pairs with pairs inside (e.g. $\left<\left<a, \left<b, c\right>\right>, \left<d, e\right>\right>$), it's tempting to sketch a binary tree for its structure, and define a representation with a inorder traversal sequence. This approach will however result in something not well-defined, since some $A_k$'s may itself be a set of pairs and one may mistake to expand pairs from $A_k$'s which should have been seen atomic, while keeping other pairs unextracted, when sketching their trees. For this, reason, it is essentially impossible to define such a concept, therefore, we shall redefine $A_0\times \cdots \times A_{k - 1}$ as their canonical representation henceforth to avoid specifying how to parse nested pairs inside into "trees" - since $\prod_{i\in \mathbb Z_k} A_i$ contains only structureless functions that is easy and clean to handle. Such a philosophy that "everything is a function" will continue in following chapters and prove beneficial.

Finally, ones must note that notations (languages) are not a part of theoretic frameworks - instead, they are simply tools for ones to typeset statements on papers, conveniently, clearly and (almost) unambiguously. Therefore, redefining them or even defining them with slight ambiguity is acceptable, as long as readers are able to decode such notations with the assistance from intuition in parsing and proper clarifications by authors. Nevertheless, successful publishments are required to define an elegant set of notations to prohibit the possibility for intended readers to misunderstand the main bulk of the theory framework.

\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Lists and tuples are essentially the same type of mathematical objects, and ones are allowed to use the notations interchangeably. Different names are introduced to emphasize the intuition that tuples have predefined lengths while components of lists tend to share same type.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	The length of a list / tuple $L$ is denoted $|L|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Verify that lengths of lists / tuples are well-defined.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	The $k$-th component of a list / tuple $L$ is denoted $L[k] = L(k - 1)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Therefore, list / tuples $L$ can be denotes by $\left<L[1], \dots, L[|k|]\right>$
	
	If a function $f$ takes a tuple $\left<x_1, \dots, x_n\right>$ as the only argument, $f(\left<x_1, \dots, x_n\right>)$ is also denoted $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ for simplicity.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	The sublist / subtuple of a list / tuple $L$ consisting of $L$'s $p$-th, $(p + 1)$-th, $\dots$, $q$-th component is denoted $L[p:q] = L|_{\{k: k\in \mathbb Z\land p \le k \le q\}}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The concatenation of a finite sequence of lists / tuple $L_1, \dots, L_k$ is denoted
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\operatorname{Concat}(L_1, \dots, L_k) = \begin{cases}
			L_k[k - S_{k - 1}] & S_{k - 1}< k \le S_{k}
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $S_k = \sum_{i = 1}^{k} |L_i|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	What advanced mathematics does is to protect ourselves against raw intuition which would lead to concepts hard to handle, or even wrong. For instance, it's trivially easy for one to understand how to list all natural numbers, possibly to pick an optimal one; however, with the scope changed to all smooth functions or random variables in a experiment, doing so gets non-trivially hard since ones are totally ignorant in how exactly such scopes are. Then, advanced but clear Hilbert spaces and axiomized probability theories will com to rescue, allowing ones to represent various functions with infinite-dimensional vectors and random stuff with deterministic functions on the outcome space assigned a probability measure, which are less intuitive at the first glance but clearer to manipulate logically with proper formalization.
	
	While formalized frameworks have transformed enormous intellectual struggling to mere deductions and calculations, it may fail to encompass all phenomenons under (current or future) interest, leading to results that is inconsistent with the reality or leaving (formalizable) truths nondeductible at all. Such frameworks could hardly be avoided, but qualified theorists are supposed to guard against them and try their best to make their ones workable in concerned topics nevertheless.
\end{remark}

\section{Algebra and Isomorphism}

Finally we will conclude our brief review on set theory with a general definition for homomorphism:

\begin{definition}\label{def:closed-system-hom}
	A simple system is a tuple $\left<S, O, R\right>$, where $S$ is a set, $O \subset \cup_{k\in \mathbb N_+} S^{(S^k)}$ is a set of operators, and $R\subset \cup_{k\in \mathbb N_+} \{\true, \false\}^{(S^k)}$ a set of relations.
	
	Tuple $\left<f, o, r\right>$ is a homomorphism from $\left<S, O, R\right>$ to $\left<S', O', R'\right>$ if $f: S\to S'$, $o: O\to O'$, $r: R\to r'$, $o, r$ are bijective, and for all $k\in \mathbb N_+$, all $\left<x_1, \dots, x_k\right>\in S^k$, all $\phi \in O$ and all $p\in R$ followings are true
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\phi\in S^{(S^k)}\iff o(\phi)\in S'^{(S'^k)}$
		\item $p\in \{\true, \false\}^{(S^k)} \iff o(p)\in \{\true, \false\}^{(S'^k)}$
		\item $\phi\in S^{(S^k)}\implies f(\phi(x_1, \dots, x_k)) = (o(\phi))(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_k))$
		\item $p\in \{\true, \false\}^{(S^k)}\implies (p(x_1, \dots, x_k) \iff (r(p))(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_k)))$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	A homomorphism $\left<f, o ,r\right>$ is an epimorphism [resp. monomorphism, isomorphism] iff $f$ is surjective [resp. injective, bijective]. If $o, r$ is clear from the context, such homomorphism can be denoted $f$ for simplicity.
	
	Two closed systems $S, T$ are isomorphic to each other (under $\left<f, o, r\right>$), notably $S\cong T$, iff there is a isomorphism between them.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	This is a generalization of homomorphism between common algebraic structures, and partially ordered sets, and readers can redefine isomorphisms between directed graphs with concepts above, for an exercise.
	
	A system is simple since only a single user-defined set $S$ is involved ($\{\true, \false\}$ is as natural as $\emptyset$ and thus not taken into account). However, it is this simplicity that prohibits us from modeling weighted graphs, Markov chains with transition probabilities and FSMs which will introduce more structured sets and then violate the closure (with respect to $S$). (One may propose that if relations / functions are themselves named ,as weights for instance so that weighted graphs are modeled. This approach, however, leaves structures of weights omitted). 
	
	Defining more complex systems will require us to handle nested homomorphisms and interdependence of different (sub)systems which is almost out of the scope of this chapter. Nevertheless, let's have a glance on the outline:
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	A closed system is a tuple $\left<\mathcal M, \Hom\right>$, where $\mathcal M$ is a set of sets and $\Hom$ is a function $\mathcal M^2\to \bigcup_{A, B \in \mathcal M} 2^{\left(B^A\right)}$ such that for all $A, B, C \in \mathcal M$:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\Hom(A, B)\subset B^A$
		\item $f\in \Hom(A, B)\land g\in \Hom(B, C) \implies g\circ f\in \Hom(A, C)$
		\item $1_A\in \Hom(A, A)$
		\item For all $L\in \mathcal M^{\mathbb N}$ and all $n\in\mathbb \overline{\mathbb N}$, $\prod_{i=0}^{n} L(i) \in \mathcal M$
		\item For all $L\in \mathcal M^{\mathbb N}$, all $n\in\mathbb \overline{\mathbb N}$, all $B\in \mathcal M$, and all $\left<f_1, \dots, f_n\right>$ where $f_i\in \prod_{i=0}^{n} \Hom(L(i), B)$ for all $1\le i\le n$, there is a unique $f\in \Hom(\prod_{i=0}^{n} L(i), B)$ such that for $f\circ(L\mapsto L[i]) = f_i$ holds for all $1\le i\le n$.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Closed system $\left<\mathcal M, \Hom\right>$ is said to be generated by set $S$ and $\Hom_0$ if $\mathcal M$ is the minimal (with respect to set inclusion) superset of $S$ and $\Hom$ is the one such that $\Hom(A, B)\supset \Hom_0(A, B)$ and is minimal for all $A, B\in\mathbb M$.
	
	Given two closed systems $\mathscr M = \left<\mathcal M, \Hom_{\mathcal M}\right>$, $\mathscr N = \left<\mathcal N, \Hom_{\mathcal N}\right>$, $\left<f, g, \alpha\right>$ is a homomorphism from $\mathscr M$ to $\mathscr N$ iff followings are true
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $f: \mathcal M\to \mathcal N$
		\item $g: \mathcal M^2\to \bigcup_{A, B\in \mathcal M} \Hom_{\mathcal N}(f(A), f(B))^{\Hom_{\mathcal M}(A, B)}$
		\item For all $A, B\in \mathcal M$, $g(A, B): \Hom_{\mathcal M}(A, B) \to \Hom_{\mathcal N}\left(f(A), f(B)\right)$
		\item $\alpha: \mathcal M\to \bigcup_{A\in\mathcal M} f(A)$
		\item For all $A$, $\alpha(A): A\to f(A)$
		\item For all $\varphi\in \Hom_{\mathcal M}(A, B)$, $\alpha(A)\circ (g(A, B))(\varphi) = \varphi\circ \alpha(B)$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	$\mathscr M$, $\mathscr N$ is isomorphic to each other, denoted $\mathscr M\cong \mathscr N$, if $f$ is bijective and for all $A, B\in\mathcal M$, $g(A, B)$'s and $\alpha(A)$'s are bijective.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	A closed system is a model for a few sets for different scopes / data types along with some relations that "matters". Such seemingly complicated can be informally rephrased as follows:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item The notation $\Hom$ is natural and intuitive to some extent.
		\item Sequential operations (denoted by composed functions) are allowed.
		\item Identities that keeps everything unchanged are trivially included - so that modeling stability and equivalence (between sequences of operators) is possible.
		\item Composed types denoted by Cartesian products are allowed so that binary operations, etc. are supported by default. Types more complex than tuple-likes (e.g. functions, unions, subsets) can also be included if one wish to.
		\item It is possible to handle composed types per-component.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	One will find the definition clear and natural with an exercise to model some computer programs you interest. (Such an exercise is not listed and it is up to the readers to teach themselves.)
	
	A homomorphism consists of three parts, that is, a $f$ to map scopes to scopes (in another possibly more simply system), a $g$ to map relations to relations, and a $\alpha$ to map concrete objects to objects. A series of conditions are imposed on homomorphisms, with most of which merely for regularity and only the last of which being an essential requirement. Readers familiar with commutative graphs may perfer the representation for $\alpha(A)\circ (g(A, B))(\varphi) = \varphi\circ \alpha(B)$ below:
	
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tikzcd}
			f(A) \arrow[r, "g(\varphi)"]& f(B)\\
			A\arrow[u, "\alpha(A)"] \arrow[r, "\varphi"] & B \arrow[u, "\alpha(B)"]
		\end{tikzcd}
	\end{center}
	
	Which is commutative. We shall use such graphs sometimes in the future for auxiliary ways to dented equivalence of composed functions for experts.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Readers with categorical backgrounds may have recognized that a closed system is essentially an concrete category, or an isomorphic fake at least. However, we are not going to introduce real categories to prevent their overwhelming abstraction from scaring innocent readers off. (Introducing genuine categories as well as existing theories built upon is fine, but useless, since we are only dealing with a few specialized instances of general categories with could be modeled with a simpler definition - meanwhile, such introduction will inevitably lead us to the hell with commutative graphs, (co)products and covariant functors, which are likely beyond the knowledge-base of most first-year graduate students.) 
	
	This concept is not used until chapters later, when we are going to discuss equivalence of a standard ticking system with simplified / regulated / abstracted versions.
	
	By the way, it leads us to an alternative definition of simple systems based upon closed ones and generic models of FSMs, etc., which the readers can give an attempt to formulate. 
	
	Open systems are almost non-processable due to unknown or hard-to-define external environments. Actually, open systems are almost always handled as closures of them and what theorists are doing is to simply pretend that some engineer-accessible parts of such closures are the entire scope.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	A $n$-ary operator on set $S$ is a function $f: S^n\to S$. For binary (2-ary) operator $\cdot$ on $S$, for all $x, y\in S$, $x\cdot y$ denotes $\cdot(x, y)$.
	
	A general algebraic structure on set $S$ is a simple system $\left<S, O, \emptyset\right>$ where $O$ is a set of $n$-ary operators. Algebraic structure $\left<S, O, \emptyset\right>$ may also be denoted by its characterizing tuple $\left<S, O\right>$.
	
	An algebraic structure on set $S$ is a tuple $\left<S, O\right>$ where $O$ is a set of $2$-ary operators.
	
	A substructure of $\left<S, O\right>$ is an algebraic structure $\left<S', O'\right>$ with $S'\subset S$ and $\forall *\in O$, there is $*' = *|_{S'}\in O'$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	The alternative notation for images of binary operators will not cause any confusion, since we won't handle boolean algebras where regarding $\cdot$ as a binary relation and a binary operator is both semantically valid.
	
	Subgroups, etc. are defined as substructures.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	An algebraic structure $\left<S, \{\cdot\}\right>$ is a group if:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\forall\left<x,y,z\right>(x, y, z\in S\implies x\cdot (y\cdot z) = (x\cdot y)\cdot z)$
		\item $\forall x (x\in S\implies\exists e(e\in S\land e\cdot x = x\cdot e = x))$ ($e$ is called the identity of the group and is unique as one can verify)
		\item $\forall x\left(x\in S\implies \exists x^{-1}(x^{-1}\in S\land x\cdot x^{-1} = x^{-1}\cdot x = e)\right)$ where $e$ is as in 2.
	\end{enumerate}
	For convenience, $\left<S, \{\cdot\}\right>$ is also denoted $\left<S, \cdot\right>$ or $S$ if it will cause no confusion. 
	
	In particular, $\left<S, \{\cdot\}\right>$ is abelian iff $\forall x\forall y(x, y\in S\implies x\cdot y = y\cdot x)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given groups $\left<S, *\right>$ and $\left<T, \cdot\right>$, function $f: G\to H$ is a homomorphism iff for all $x, y\in S$, $f(x)\cdot f(y) = x*y$. A homomorphism $f$ is a epimorphism [resp. monomorphism, isomorphism] iff $f$ is surjective [resp. injective, bijective]. Two groups are isomorphic iff there is a isomorphism between them.
	
	Let $e_T$ denote the identity in group $T$, the kernel of $f$ in $G$ is denoted $\ker f = f^{-1}(e_T)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	This is a restatement of Definition \ref{def:closed-system-hom} as one can verify. The same applies for rings and modules.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Let $\left<G, \cdot\right>$ be a group, and $H$ a subgroup of $G$, $H$ is normal in $G$ iff $\{g \cdot h\cdot g^{-1}: g\in G\land h\in H\}\subset H$. 
	
	A left [resp. right] coset of $H$ in $G$ at $a\in H$ is $aH\{a\cdot h: h\in H\}$ [resp. $Ha = \{h\cdot a: h\in H\}$].
	
	The quotient group of $G$ and $H$ normal in $G$ is a group $\left<G / H, \cdot'\right>$ such that $G / H = {aH: a\in G}$ is the set of all left cosets of $H$ in $G$ and $aH\cdot' bH = (a\cdot b)H$ for all $a, b \in G$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The definition above requires a series of lemmas to justify, but we are not explicitly reference any of them as they are out of the scope.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
	Given a homomorphism $G\to H$ between groups, $\ker f$ is normal in $G$ and furthermore $G / \ker f\cong \im f$, where $\ker f$ and $\im f$ are subgroups of $G$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}
	An algebraic structure $\left<S, \{+, \cdot\}\right>$ is a ring if:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\left<S, +\right>$ is a abelian group
		\item $\forall \left<x, y, x\right>(x, y, z\in S) \implies (x + y) \cdot z = x\cdot z + y\cdot z$
		\item $\forall \left<x, y, x\right>(x, y, z\in S) \implies x\cdot (x + y) = z\cdot x + z\cdot y$
		\item $\exists 1(1\in S\land \forall x(x\in S\implies 1\cdot x = x\cdot 1 = x))$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Where $0$ is the additive identity and $1$ is the multiplicative identity of $\left<S, \{+, \cdot\}\right>$.
	
	A ring is commutative iff $\forall x\forall y(x, y\in S\implies x\cdot y = y\cdot x)$, is a integral domain iff $\forall x\forall y(x, y\in S\implies (x\cdot y = 0\implies x = 0\lor y = 0))$ where $0$ is the identity of $\left<S, +\right>$, is a division ring iff $\left<S - \{0\}, \{+, \cdot\}\right>$ is a group, and is a field iff it is a commutative division ring.
	
	Likewise, one can denote $\left<S, \{+, \cdot\}\right>$ with $\left<S, +, \cdot\right>$ or $S$ for convenience as long as no confusion will result.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	$+$ and $\cdot$ is not distinguishable with notation $\left<S, \{+, \cdot\}\right>$, but $\left<S, +, \cdot\right>$ will clarify the ordering.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given rings $\left<S, \#, \&\right>$ and $\left<T, +, \cdot\right>$, function $f: G\to H$ is a homomorphism iff for all $x, y\in S$, $f(x)\cdot f(y) = x\& y$ and $f(x) + f(y) = x \# y$. Isomorphism, etc. are defined likewise.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a ring $\left<R, +_R, \cdot\right>$, an abelian group $\left<M, +_M\right>$ is a $R$-module under scalar multiplication $f: R\times M \to M$ iff for all $r, s\in R$ and $u, v \in M$, followings are true
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $f(r, u +_M v) = f(r, u) +_M f(r, v)$
		\item $f(r +_R s, u) = f(r, u) +_M f(s, u)$
		\item $f(r\cdot s, u) = f(r, f(s, u))$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	If $R$ is a division ring, $\left<M, +_M\right>$ is a $R$-vector space under $f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	It is clear that modules are generalizations for vector spaces if one denote $f(r, u)$ by $ru$. $+_R$ and $+_M$ will share the notation $+$ in following texts if no ambiguity will result.
\end{remark}

\section{Linear Algebra}

\begin{definition}
	Given a vector space $\left<V, +\right>$, a subset $S\subset V$, the subspace generated by $S$ is $\Span S = \cap\{U: U\subset V\land S\subset U\land U \text{ is a subspace of $V$}\}$.
	
	An vector space $V$ is finite dimensional if there is a finite set $S$ such that $\Span S = V$.
	
	Let $0_R$ denote the additive identity of ring $\left<R, +, \cdot\right>$, $0_V$ denote the identity of $R$-vector space $\left<V, +\right>$ with scalar multiplication $f$, a subset $S\in V$ is linearly independent iff for all $n\in \mathbb N_+$ and lists $C$ on $R$ and $L$ on $S$, both with length $n$, $f(C[1], L[1]) + \cdot + f(C[n], L[n]) = 0_V \iff C[1] = \cdot = C[n] = 0_R$. Any linearly independent set $S$ such that $\Span S = V$ is a basis of $V$, and the cardinal $|S|$ is the number of dimensions of $V$ when well-defined, denote $\dim V$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	The definition, again, requires numerous lemmas to justify, but qualified readers should feel them intuitive.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Definition for matrices, ranks, etc. are omitted for simplicity, what we shall define is following notations:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $A^t$, for transposition
		\item $A^*$, for adjoint operators
		\item $A_{ij}$, for the element at the $i$-th row and $j$-th column of $A$. Note that the numbering of rows / columns starts from 1.
		\item $A_{i\cdot}$, $A_{\cdot j}$ for the matrix consisting of elements from the $i$-th row / $j$-th column of $A$, with a corresponding shape.
		\item $\rank{T}$ for the rank of object $T$. 
	\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\section{Elementary Analysis}

\begin{definition}
	The set $\mathbb R$ of real numbers is an set equipotent with $2^{\mathbb N}$ and $\mathbb Q\subset R$. The closed system for real numbers is $\left<\mathbb R, \{+, \cdot\}, \{\le\}\right>$ such that
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\left<\mathbb R, +, \cdot\right>$ is a field with additive identity $0$ and multiplicative identity $1$
		\item $\left<\mathbb R, <\right>$ is linearly ordered
		\item For all $x, y, z\in \mathbb R$, $x < y\implies x + z < y + z$
		\item For all $x, y, z\in \mathbb R$, $x < y \land 0 < z\implies x\cdot z < y\cdot z$
		\item For all $S\subset \mathbb R$, let $S' = \{x: x\in \mathbb R\land \forall y(y\in S\implies y \le x)\}$ be the set of upper bounds of $S$, then, there exist a unique minimal member $s = \sup S\in S'$ of $S'$ such that $\forall z(z\in S'\implies s \le z)$.
	\end{enumerate} 
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	We are not going to explicitly specify how to construct all real numbers, since the structure with respect to $+$, $\cdot$ and $<$ is unique and what real numbers are are transparent in analysis.
	
	Relation operators $<$, $>$ and $\ge$ are defined as the conventionally are.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Let $a, b\in \mathbb R$, $[a, b] = \{x: a\le x\land x\le b\}$, $(a, b)$, $(a, b]$ and $[a, b)$ are finite intervals with length $|b - a|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Intervals involving infinities are defined as how they conventionally are.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Let $f$ be a function with $\dom f\in \mathbb R$, $f$ converges to  $l$ at $x_0$, denoted $f(x)\to l$ as $x\to x_0$ or $\lim_{x\to x_0} f(x) = l$, provided that $\forall \varepsilon(\epsilon \ge 0\implies\exists \delta\forall x(\delta > 0\land (|x - x_0|\le \delta\land x\in \dom f \implies |f(x) - l|<\varepsilon)))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	(Uniform) continuity, derivative, and limits involving infinity are defined as they conventionally are. Note that functions are continuous on $I$ iff $f(x)\to f(x_0)$ as $x\to x_0$ and $f$ is defined on $I$.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given interval $I$, $C^0I$ denotes the set of functions such that $f^{(n)}$, the $n$-th derivative of $f$, is continuous on $I$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Riemann integrals shall be defined.
\end{remark}

\section{Probability Theory}

\begin{definition}
	A set $\mathcal S$ of sets is a $\sigma$-algebra on set $S$ iff
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\mathcal S\subset 2^S$
		\item $\emptyset \in \mathcal S$
		\item For all $A \in \mathcal S$, $S - A \in \mathcal S$
		\item For all sequences $\left<A_0, A_1, \dots\right>$ on $\mathcal S$, $\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb N} A_i\in \mathcal S$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	A sigma generated by a set $P$ is the minimal (with respect to set inclusion) one containing $P$. The Borel sets on $\mathbb R^n$ denoted $\mathcal R_n$ is generated by all open sets in $\mathbb R^n$.
	
	A measurable space is a pair $\left<S, \mathcal S\right>$ with $\mathcal S$ being a $\sigma$-algebra on set $S$.
	
	$\mu$ is a measure on $\left<S, \mathcal S\right>$ (or on $S$ for simplicity) iff
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\mu: \mathcal S\to [0, +\infty]$
		\item $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$
		\item For all sequences $\left<A_0, A_1, \dots\right>$ on $\mathcal S$, $\sum_{i\in\mathbb N}\mu(A_i) = \mu(\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb N} A_i)$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	A function $S\to T$ is measureable with respect to $\left<S, \mathcal S\right>$ and $\left<T, \mathcal T\right>$ if for each $A\in \mathcal T$, $f^{-1}(A) \in \mathcal S$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Lebesgue integral $\int_A f(x)\mathrm{d}\mu$ shall be defined.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	A probability space is a tuple $\left<\Omega, \mathcal H, \mathbb P\right>$, such that
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\left<\Omega, \mathcal H\right>$ is a measureable space;
		\item $\mathbb P$ is a measure space on $\left<\Omega, \mathcal H\right>$;
		\item $\mathbb P(\Omega) = 1$.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Members of $\Omega$ are call outcomes, and members of $\mathcal H$ are events. Two events are independent iff $\mathbb P(A\cap B) = \mathbb P(A) \mathbb P(B)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Classical properties for the probability measure $\mathbb P$ like finite additivity are not restated.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a probability space $\left<\Omega, \mathcal H, \mathbb P\right>$ and a measureable space $\left<S, \mathcal S\right>$, $X: \Omega \to S$ is a random variable.
	
	If $X: \Omega\to \mathbb R^d$, $F_X: \left<x_1, \dots, x_d\right> \to \mathbb P\{\omega: (X(\omega))[i] < x_i\}$ for $x_1, \\in \mathbb R$ is the cdf of $X$.	
	
	Random variables $X_1, \dots: \omega\to \mathbb R$ with cdf $F_{X_1}, \dots$ are independent iff $F_{\left<X_1, \dots\right>}(x_1, \dots) = F_{X_1}(x_2)\cdot F_{X_2}(x_2)\cdots$ for all $x, y\in \mathcal R$.
	
	Expectation of random variable $X$ is $\mathbb EX = \int_\Omega X(\omega) \mathbb P(\mathrm d\omega)$, variance of $X$ is $\Var X = \mathbb E(X - \mathcal E X)^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Note that $S$ could be virtually any set, like $\{0, 1\}$, $\{\true, \false\}$, $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb R^n$, or even $\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}$, which will be further exploited by the next definition
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a probability space $\left<\Omega, \mathcal H, \mathbb P\right>$, a measureable space $\left<S, \mathcal S\right>$, and a linearly ordered set $\left<\mathbb T, \le\right>$, a stochastic process with state space $S$ is a random variable $P: \Omega\to S^{\mathbb T}$.
	
	A stopping time with respect to $P$ is a random variable $T: \Omega\to \mathbb T$ such that for each $t\in \mathbb T$ there is a function $f: S^{\{u: u< t\}}\to \mathbb T$ such that $T(\omega) = f(u\to (X(\omega))(u))$.
	
	If $S$ is linearly ordered by $\leq$, $P$ is a submartingale [resp. supermartingale, martingale] iff for all stopping time $T\le U$, $(\mathbb EP)(t)\leq  (\mathbb EP)(u)$ [resp. $(\mathbb EP)(u)\leq  (\mathbb EP)(t)$, $(\mathbb EP)(t) =  (\mathbb EP)(u)$]
\end{definition}

		\item For one or two examples you have given, find a function $f: \mathbb N\to \mathbb R$ such that $(S_k - T_k)^2 / f(n)$ is an martingale. (Or give a proof of non-existence.)
	\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Computer Model And Pseudo Code}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{type} is a set, whose members are referred to as \defbf{value of type}[values of the type]. A set of (not necessarily disjoint) types is a \defbf{type space}.
	
	A \defbf{variable name space} is a set.
	
	Given a type $T$, variable name space $N$, the \defbf{variable space} on $\mathcal T$ and $\mathcal N$ is a tuple $\left\{\left<n, v, t\right>: n\in N \land t \in T \land v\in t\right\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{stack frame} is a subset a variable space where equivalence of first elements of two tuples in such a subset implies the equivalence of the two tuples.
	
	A \defbf{computer state space} is a set of finite list of stack frames, whose elements are \defbf{computer state}[computer states].
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	The definition implies that names must be unique within a stack frame.
	
	Unlike conventional stack frame created with function calls, stack frames in our definition are essentially counterparts for code blocks, which allows ones to define variables with duplicated names to "hid" outsider ones.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{statement} is a unary operator on a computer state space.
	
	A piece of \defbf{pseudo code} is a list of statements.
	
	The execution of pseudo code $P$ is $E = P[1] \circ \cdots P[|P|]$. The output of $P$ at $S$ is $E(S)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a computer state $S$ and a variable name $n$, $\operatorname{VariableFrame}(S, n)$ denotes the largest integer $k$ such that $S[k]$ contains a variable named $n$, $\operatorname{Variable}(S, n)$ denotes the variable with name $n$ in $S[\operatorname{VariableFrame}(S, l)]$
	
	Given a computer state $S$ and an expression $e$, $\operatorname{Eval}(S, n)$ is the value of $e$ at $S$, whose precise definition is omitted.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{rvalue} is a mathematical object, and a \defbf{lvalue} is the name of a variable.
	
	An \defbf{assign statement} is a statement defined by a pair $(l, r)$ and written as $l \rightarrow r$ in pseudo codes, such that maps a computer state $S$ to $S'$ such that
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $|S'| = |S|$
		\item $S'[k] = S[k]$ whenever $k \ne s$
		\item $S'[s] = \left(S[s] - \{\left<l, v, t\right>\}\right) \cup \{\left<l, r, t\right>\}$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Where $s =  \operatorname{VariableFrame}(S, l)$.
	
	A \defbf{compound statement} is a statement denoted by a list $L$ of statements and written as $\{L[1], \dots, L[n]\}$, and the execution of a compound statement equals the execution of
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\operatorname{Concat}\left((S \mapsto \operatorname{Concat}(S, (())), L, S\mapsto S[1:|S| - 1])\right)
	\end{equation*}
	
	A \defbf{conditional statement} is a statement denoted by a pair $(C, T)$ and written as $\mathtt{if}C: T;$. The execution of such a statement at $S$ is equivalent to the execution of $C\circ T$ if $\operatorname{Eval}(S, C) = \mathcal{true}$, or $C$ if $\operatorname{Eval}(S, C) = \mathcal{false}$.
	
	A \defbf{branch statement} is a statement denoted by a tuple $(C, T, F)$ and written as $\mathtt{if}C: T; \mathtt{else} F;$. The execution of such a statement at $S$ is equivalent to the execution of $C\circ T$ if $\operatorname{Eval}(S, C) = \mathcal{true}$, or $C\circ F$ if $\operatorname{Eval}(S, C) = \mathcal{false}$.
	
	A \defbf{do-until statement} is a statement denoted by a pair $(B, C)$ and written as $\mathtt{do} B \mathtt{until} C;$, whose execution at is equivalent to the execution of $\lim_{n\to\infty} L_k$ where
	
	\begin{equation*}
		L_k = \begin{cases}
			B \circ C& k = 0\\
			L_{k - 1} & \operatorname{Eval}((\circ L_{k - 1})(S), C) = \mathtt{false}\\
			\operatorname{Concat}(L_{k - 1}, (B, C)) & \text{otherwise}
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	
	A \defbf{while statement} is a statement denoted by a pair $(B, C)$ and written as $\mathtt{while} C: B;$, whose execution at $S$ is equivalent to the execution of the pseudo code denoted by $\mathtt{if} C:\mathtt{do}B;\mathtt{until} C$.
	
	A \defbf{for statement} is a statement denoted by a tuple $(I, C, J, B)$ and written as $\mathtt{for} I; C; J: B;$, whose execution at $S$ is equivalent to the execution of the pseudo code denoted by $I\mathtt{while} C: BJ$.
	
	A \defbf{label} is a statement denoted by a variable name $n$ and written $n:$.
	
	\defbf{goto statement}s, \defbf{break statement}s and \defbf{continue statement}s are defined as you wish it to be. (You may have to modify existing definitions or make them "code modifiers", which is left as an optional exercise. Also note that our continue / break statements behave like ones in Java programs)
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	We shall adopt the pseudo code convention in CLRS 4e, with minor modification for symbolic consistence. The definition above is basically a restatement of such convention, slightly less rigorous than expected though.
	
	Ones may question if it is reasonable not to formalize the syntax of our pseudo code convention as rigor as other mathematical objects. The answer is fine, anyway, we are not such a enthusiastic in formalism that we want to formalize even typesetting structures simply adopted for manual comprehension instead of being a part in mathematical reliance, and reads should always be able to translate such structures into mathematical definitions (much lengthy or complicated though), and the translation is unique (up to some isomorphism at least).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	We could have begun with some assembly-like pseudo codes (probably an adapted version for the JVM instruction set) for the maximal degree of rigor or cleanness, and algorithmic analysis would benefit from it. However, practices in researches on game mechanism have proven the viability and usefulness of structured codes and we'll follow such conventions. 
	
	Pure Java language is basically well-defined and its usage is favorable in specific language-dependent topics, but it is not used for general mechanism descriptions due to its inability to have concise mathematical concepts easily embedded in.
\end{remark}

	\texttt{switch} statements are not included for its rare usage in server-side logic.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	 A \defbf{unit group} $\left<U\right>$ is the abelian group generated by $U$, the {\bf{elementary unit set}}, all of whose elements has an infinite order. An element in an unit group is a \defbf{unit}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	An \defbf{united algebra}[united algebra on $A$ with unit $u$], notably $A_u$, is an algebraic structure of ordered pairs $<m, u>$, where $m \in A$ , $u\in U$ for some unit group $U$ and $A$ is an algebraic structure. An element in an united algebra is a \defbf{united value}. Its left and right component are called its \defbf{magnitude} $\mu(<m, u>)$ and \defbf{unit} $\upsilon(<m, u>)$ respectively.
	
	If addition and / or abstraction are defined on $A$, they are automatically defined on $A_u$ by $<m_1, u> \pm <m_2, u> = <m_1 \pm m_2, u>$.
	
	If multiplication is defined on $A$, it is also defined between elements in two possibly different united algebras on $A$ as $<m_1, u_1><m_2, u_2> = <m_1 m_2, u_1 u_2>$, where $u_1$ and $u_2$ is multiplied with the binary operator on $U$. If there is a multiplicative inverse $m^{-1}$ of $m$ in $A$, $<m, u>^{-1} = <m^{-1}, u^{-1}>$
	
	For $u_1$ and $u_2$ in the same unit group and $A$ with a multiplicative identity $1_A$, the \defbf{transition unit}[transition unit from $A_{u_1}$ to $A_{u_2}$] is $<1_A, u_1^{-1}u_2>$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	United algebra is not an algebra being "united" but an algebraic structure with units.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Note that addition is closed on $A_u$ but multiplication is not necessarily.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	If the unit of an united value is the identity of underlying unit group, it is regarded to equal its magnitude for convenience. If the magnitude of an united value is the additive identity and multiplicative zero, then one is free to take any value for its unit to make expressions involved sensible.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given an algebraic structures $F$ on which addition and multiplication is defined and with an additive and multiplicative identity, two units $u_x$, $u_v$ in the same unit group $U$, the \defbf{entity state space}[$n$-dimensional entity state space on $F$ with unit $u_x$ and $u_v$], denoted $S_{F, u_x, u_v, n}$ and abbreviated {\bf{state space}}, is the set of ordered pairs of two $n$-tuples, with the left component in $F_{u_x}^n$ and the right one in $F_{u_v}^n$.
	\begin{equation*}
		S_{F, u_x, u_v, n} = \{ <x, v> : x \in F_{u_x}^n \land v \in F_{u_v}^n\}
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	As a idealized model, we define the \defbf{continuous entity state space}[$n$-dimensional simple continuous entity state space] as $C_n = S_{\mathbb{R}, 1_U, 1_U, n}$, where $\mathbb{R}$ is the field of real numbers with conventional addition and multiplication and $1_U$ is the identity in an unit group $U$. Note that $C_n$ is the set of ordered pairs of $n$-dimensional real vectors.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	The definition has imposed a minimized requirement on qualified $F$'s. This decision, although may result in a overly high level of abstraction, allows the floating point arithmetic which is done on real computers and Minecraft instances to our framework.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	One is free to believe $F_u^n = (F_u)^n$ or $(F^n)_u$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Two distinct united algebraic structures are involved in the definition to prohibit direct addition / subtraction between objects with different dimensions (in other words, for different physical concepts). Multiplication is the only natural legitimate way in which they interact.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	An element in a state space is an \defbf{entity state}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The left component of an entity state $S$ is the position component of the it, denoted $P(S)$. The right component of $S$ it the velocity component of it, denoted $V(S)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Any ordered pair of $3$-dimensional real vector, $<(x, y, z), (v_x, v_y, v_z)>$ with $x, y, z, v_x, v_y, v_z \in \mathbb{R}$ is an entity state in $C_3$.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a state space $S$, a \defbf{motion operator}[motion operator on $S$] is a function $f: S\to S$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a $n$-dimensional state space $S$ on $F$ with units $u_x$ and $u_v$, there are three families of \defbf{basic elementary motion operator}[basic elementary motion operators], defined as follows
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item {\defbf{movement operator}[Movement operators]} $\{M_{t}: M_{t_0}<x, v> = <x + vt, v> \land t \in T\}$;
		\item {\defbf{acceleration operator}[Acceleration operators]} $\{A_{I}: A_{I}<x, v> = <x, v + I> \land I\in F_{u_v}^n\}$;
		\item {\defbf{drag operator}[Drag operators]} $\{D_k: D_k<x, v> = <x, kv> \land k\in U\}$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Where $T = \{<m, u_v^{-1}u_x>: m\in F\}$ and $U = \{<m, u_x^{-1}u_v>: m\in F\}$.
	
	An motion operator is \defbf{elementary motion operator}[elementary] if it is the composition of some finite lists of these operators. 
	
	Furthermore, an elementary motion operator is \defbf{simple motion operator}[simple] if there exists one of its factorization as a finite composition of basic elementary motion operators containing at most one movement operator.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Component-wise addition and scalar multiplication is implied in the definition.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	One would prefer to name acceleration and drag operators as additive and decaying operators respectively.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
	The set of all elementary motion operators except $D_0$ on a state space on a division ring is a group under function composition.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
	We denote such group as $\mathcal{E}_S$, where $S$ is the state space mentioned.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
	Closure and associativity is trivial, and the identity is trivially $M_0 = D_0 = D_1$. For a inversibility, consider the inverse of basic elementary motion operators:
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\begin{matrix}
			M_{t}^{-1} = M_{-t}&
			A_{I}^{-1} = A_{-I}&
			D_{k}^{-1} = D_{k^{-1}}
		\end{matrix}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Therefore, given an elementary motion operator $f: f_1 \circ \cdots \circ f_n$, where $f_1, \dots, f_n$ are basic elementary motion operators, its inverse is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		f^{-1} = f_n^{-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_1^{-1}
	\end{equation*}
	
	As one can verify.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	We are adopting left composition in this book, that is, $(f \circ g)(x) = g(f(x))$. It is so that the visual order of a long sequence of motion operators will be consist with the order in which they are applied, as you may have seen in existing articles / discussions.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a state space $S$, an \defbf{inspect function} is a function $f: S \times S \to \{0, 1\}$ such that:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $x = y \implies f(x, y) = 1$;
		\item $f(x, y) = f(y, x)$;
		\item $f(x, y) = 1 \land f(y, z) = 1 \implies f(x, z) = 1$.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Two entity states $S_0, T_0$ is \defbf{$f$-equivalent} if $f(S_0, T_0) = 1$, denoted $S_0 \overset{f}{\sim} T_0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	It's easy to see that $f$-equivalence is an equivalence relation.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	The \defbf{natural inspect function} is defined as $\nu : (x, y) \mapsto x = y$. It's easy to see that if $\nu$ maps a pair of entity states to 1, so do any other inspect function. For comparisons on specific components, we have the {\bf{natural inspect function for position}} $\nu_p: (x, y) \mapsto P(x) = P(y)$ and the one for velocity $\nu_v: (x, y)\mapsto V(x) = V(y)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	An \defbf{application of inspect function} $f$ on $S$ is a function defined by tuple $<m, n, f>$ with $m, n \in \mathbb{N}$ that maps an entity state $S_0\in S$ along with any two lists of motion operators, $f_1, \dots, f_M$ and $g_1, \dots, g_N$ with $m > M$ and $n > N$, to a boolean value
	
	\begin{equation*}
		<m, n, f>: (S_0, f_1, \dots, f_M, g_1, \dots, g_N) \mapsto (f_1 \circ \cdots \circ f_M)(S_0) = (g_1 \circ \cdots \circ g_M)(S_0)
	\end{equation*}
	
	Given the lists of motion operators defined above, $\underline{f}$, a function from $S_0$ to $\{0, 1\}$ defined by tuple $<M, N, f>$, is the \defbf{tail application}[tail application of $f$ on $f_1, \dots, f_M$ and $g_1, \dots, g_N$].
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Tail applications test the ultimate equivalence of two sequences of motion operators.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given an inspect function application $A$ and state space $S$, two sequence of motion operators, $f_1, \dots, f_M$ and $g_1, \dots, g_N$, are {\bf{$A$-equivalent}} provided that
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\forall s (s \in S \implies\alpha(s, f_1, \dots, f_M, g_1, \dots, g_N) = 1)
	\end{equation*}
	
	In particular, if $A$ is the a tail application on $f_1, \dots, f_M$ and $g_1, \dots, g_N$ of $f$, the two lists are \defbf{$f$-tail equivalent} If $F = \nu$, the two lists are simply \defbf{tail equivalent}; if $F = \nu_p$, the two list are {\bf{positionally tail equivalent}}; and if $F = \nu_v$, the two list are {\bf{velocitically tail equivalent}}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
	For any inspect function application $A$, $A$-equivalence is an equivalence relation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
	If both lists contains exactly one motion operator, say $f$ and $g$ respectively, then they said to be $A$-equivalent as this notation won't cause confusion.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	The existence of $A$-equivalence classes would follow. We take the notation $F / A$ for the set of all $A$-equivalent classes in a set of motion operators $F$ and the notation of $\overline{f}$ for the class containing $f$.
\end{remark}

\end{proof}

\section{Operators On Fields}

\begin{definition}
	The $n$-th power of an motion operator $f$ is the composition of $n$ $f$'s, denoted $f^n$. We define $f^0$ as the identity mapping on the implied state space and $f^{-n}$ be $(f^{-1})^n$ as long as $f^{-1}$ exists.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:vel-formula-gen}
	For any simple motion operator $f$ on $d$-dimensional state space $S$ on field $F$ with units $u_x$ and $u_v$, there exist some $k \in F_1$ and $b\in F_{u_v}^d$ such that at least one of following propositions would hold:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item For all $n\in \mathbb N$ (or $n\in \mathbb Z$ if $f$ is bijective), the $n$-th power of $f$ is velocitically tail equivalent to any motion operator that maps $<x, v>$ to 
		
		\begin{equation*}
			<x', k^nv + \frac{b(1 - k^n)}{1-k}>
		\end{equation*}
		
		\item For all $n\in \mathbb Z$, the $n$-th power of $f$ is velocitically tail equivalent to any motion operator that maps $<x, v>$ to 
		
		\begin{equation*}
			<x', v + nb>
		\end{equation*}
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Where $x' \in F_{u_x}^d$ and needn't hold the same value for all $n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}

\section{Operators On Floating Point Sets}

\begin{definition}
	The {\bf{finite IEEE floating point set of $e$ exponential bits and $s$ significant bits}} is a subset of $\mathbb{R}$
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal{F}(e, s) = \{m \times 2^k: m, k \in \mathbb{Z} \land |m| < 2^s \land  2^{-2^{e - 1} + 2 - s} \le k \le 2^{2^{e - 1} - s}  \}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $e, s \in \mathbb{N}_+\cup \{\infty\}$. In particular, the {\bf{upper unbounded floating point set with $e$ exponential bits and $s$ significant bits}} is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\hat{\mathcal{F}}(e, s) = \{m \times 2^k: m, k \in \mathbb{Z} \land |m| < 2^ s \land k \ge 2^{-2^{e - 1} + 2 - s}\}
	\end{equation*}
	
	And the {\bf{(bidirectional) unbounded floating point set with $s$ significant bits}} is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal{F}(s) = \mathcal{F}(\infty, s) = \{m \times 2^k: m, k \in \mathbb{Z} \land |m| < 2^s\}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Any set in the three families states above is a \defbf{floating point set}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Infinities and NaNs are excluded from the definition for regularity of arithmetic operations.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{order function} $\mathcal o_{e, s}$ on $\mathcal{F}(e, s)$ or $\hat{\mathcal{F}}(e, s)$ is defined as
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal o_{e, s}(x) = -s + \max\left\{\left\lfloor \log_2 |x| \right\rfloor, 2 - 2^{e - 1}\right\}
	\end{equation*}
	
	And $x$ is said to have an \defbf{order} $k$ in $\mathcal F(e, s)$ or $\hat{\mathcal F}(e, s)$ if $\mathcal o_{e, s}(x) = k$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a floating point set $\mathcal{F}$, a \defbf{rounding function}[rounding function on $\mathcal{F}$] is a monotonically increasing function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathcal{F}$ such that $\forall x(x\in \mathcal F \implies f(x) = x)$.
	
	In particular, the \defbf{default rounding function}[default rounding function on $\mathcal{F}$] with $s$ significant bits is defined as follows
	
	\begin{equation*}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(x) = 
		\begin{cases}
			x & x \in \mathcal{F}\\
			\argmin\limits_{t\in \mathcal{F}} |x - t| & |\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}, x)| = 1\\
			\argmin\limits_{t\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}, x)} \left(|t| \cdot 2^{-\mathcal o_{e, s}(x)} \mod 2\right) & |\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}, x)| = 2
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}, x) = \{ t : |x - t| = \min\{|x - u|: u\in \mathcal{F}\}\}$, the set of points $t$ in $\mathcal{F}$ at which $|x - t|$ attains the minimum.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	$\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}$ is defined as how IEEE 754 defines the rounding to nearest mode, but we have to verify that $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}$ is well defined, that is, always producing a unique value in $\mathcal{{F}}$, which is left as an exercise.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
	Any rounding function $f$ preserves sign: $f(x) \ge 0 \iff x \ge 0$ and $f(x) \le 0 \iff x \le 0$ and $f(0) = 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Note that $0$ is contained in every floating point set and the rest is trivial by that $f$ is monotonically increasing.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:round-bound}
	Given $\mathcal F(e, s)$ and $x\in \mathbb R$ with $\inf \mathcal F(e, s) \le x \le \sup \mathcal F(e, s)$ and $x\ne 0$, there is a bound for its default rounding function:
	
	\begin{align*}
		\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) &\ge \min \left\{\frac{\left\lfloor 2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}x\right\rfloor}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}}, x - 2^{1 - 2^{e - 1} - s}\right\}\\
		\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) &\le \max \left\{\frac{\left\lceil 2^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}x\right\rceil}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}}, x + 2^{1 - 2^{e - 1} - s}\right\}
	\end{align*}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Clearly,
	
	\begin{equation*}
		m = \frac{\left\lfloor 2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}x\right\rfloor}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}} \le x \le \frac{\left\lceil 2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}x\right\rceil}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}}  = M
	\end{equation*}
	
	If $\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) = x$, the theorem trivially holds.
	
	If $\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) > x$ and
	$\log_2 |x| \le 2 - 2^{e - 1}$, note that $M \in \mathcal F(e, s)$ and $M \ge x + 2^{1 - 2^{e - 1} - s}$, if $\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) > M$, $|\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) - x| = \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) - x > M - x = |M - x|$ would hold, violating the definition of $\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}$ which requires $\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x)$ to be the an element $\mathcal F(e, s)$ closest to $x$. Thus $m < x < \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) < M$.
	
	If $\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) > x$ but
	$\log_2 |x| < 2 - 2^{e - 1}$, verify that there is an element $y \in \mathcal F(e, s)$ such that $x \le y \le  x + 2^{1 - 2^{e - 1} - s}$ and that $x + 2^{1 - 2^{e - 1} - s} \ge M$ and done.
	
	Apply similar proof to the case with $\mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x) < x$ to complete the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:round-stability}
	Given $\mathcal F(e, s)$ and $x, h\in \mathbb R$ with $x \ne 0$ and $x + h \ne 0$ and $\min \mathcal F(e, s) \le x, x + h \le \max \mathcal F(e, s)$, there holds
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\left| \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)} (x + h) - \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)} (x) \right| \le \max \left\{\left|h + 2^{1 - s + \left\lceil \log_2 \max\{|x|, |x + h|\} \right\rceil}\right|, 2^{2 -2^{e - 1} - s}\right\}
	\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Suppose $h \ge 0$ first. Apply Theorem \ref{thm:round-bound} at $x$ and $x + h$ to obtain bounds for $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}(e, s)}(x)$ and $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal F(e, s)}(x + h)\}$, and for $|x|, |x + h| \ge 2^{2 - 2^{e - 1} - s}$ verify that
	
	\begin{align*}
		\left| \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)} (x + h) - \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)} (x) \right| 
		& \le \left|  \frac{\left\lceil 2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x + h| \right\rceil}(x + h)\right\rceil}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x + h| \right\rceil}} - \frac{\left\lfloor 2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}x\right\rfloor}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}}  \right| \\
		& \le \left|  \frac{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x + h| \right\rceil}(x + h) + 1}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x + h| \right\rceil}} - \frac{2^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}x - 1}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}}  \right| \\
		& =  \left| h + \frac{1}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x + h| \right\rceil}} + \frac{1}{2 ^{s - \left\lceil \log_2 |x| \right\rceil}}  \right| \\
		& \le \left|h + 2^{1 - S + \left\lceil \log_2 \max\{|x|, |x + h|\} \right\rceil}\right|
	\end{align*}\\
	
	And that $\left|h + 2^{1 - S + \left\lceil \log_2 \max\{|x|, |x + h|\} \right\rceil}\right| \ge 2^{2 -2^{e - 1} - s}$.
	
	For $|x|$ or $|x + h|$ smaller than $2^{2 - e^{e - 1} - s}$, verify that
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\left| \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)} (x + h) - \mathcal R_{\mathcal F(e, s)} (x) \right| \le 2^{2 -2^{e - 1} - s}
	\end{equation*}
	
	And that $\left|h + 2^{1 - S + \left\lceil \log_2 \max\{|x|, |x + h|\} \right\rceil}\right| \le 2^{2 -2^{e - 1} - s}$.
	
	Then apply similar routine for $h\le 0$ to complete the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
	Given a floating point set $\mathcal F$ and rounding function $f$, the \defbf{basic algebraic structure}[basic algebraic structure on $\mathcal F$ for $f$] is $\mathcal{F}$ along with three arithmetic operators defined below, 
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Addition: $x +_f y = f(x + y)$
		\item Subtraction: $x -_f y = f(x - y)$
		\item Multiplication: $x \times_f y = f(x \times y)$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Where $+, -, \times$ are conventional arithmetic operators on $\mathbb{R}$.
	
	If $f = \mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}$, the algebraic structure is \defbf{default algebraic structure}[default], denoted $\mathcal{F}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	We would use common algebraic operators (e.g. using $+$ instead of $+_f$ and omitting multiplicative operators) if they would cause no confusion. Also note that the default algebraic structure share the same notation as its underlying set.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Division is excluded from the definition as we are not going to handle it and it is harder to define to ensure closure.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{simple arithmetic procedure}[$n$-step simple arithmetic procedure on] a subset of $\mathbb R$ is a pair $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$ such that for some $n\in \mathbb N$:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\mathcal N \in \mathcal F^{n + 1}$;
		\item $\mathcal O \in (\mathbb R^{\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}})^{n}$;
	\end{enumerate}
	
	In particular, the arithmetic procedure is \defbf{realistic simple arithmetic procedure}[realistic on a floating point set $\mathcal F$ and $f$] if $f$ is a rounding function on $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal O \in \{+_f, -_f, \times_f\}^n$.
	
	The \defbf{sub-procedure}[$k$-th (head) sub-procedure of $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$], denoted $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>[1:n]$, is obtained by replacing in $\mathcal N$, $\mathcal O>$ with the $\mathcal N[1:n + 1]$ and $\mathcal N[1:n]$ respectively.
\end{definition}	

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{value of simple arithmetic procedure}[value] of $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$, denoted $\operatorname{val} <\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$, is recursively defined as the value of
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\begin{cases}
			<(\mathcal{N}[1] \mathcal{O}[1] \mathcal{N}[2], \mathcal{N}[3], \dots, \mathcal{N}[n + 1]), (\mathcal{O}[2], \dots, \mathcal{O}[n])> & n > 0\\
			\mathcal {N}_1 & n = 0
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $\mathcal{N}[1] \mathcal{O}[1] \mathcal{N}[2]$ is the image of $\mathcal{N}[1], \mathcal{N}[2]$ under operator $\mathcal{O}[1]$.
	
	For any integer $i$ such that $i \le i \le n$, the \defbf{intermediate value}[$i$-th intermediate value] of $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$,  notably $\operatorname{immval_i}<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$, is the value of the $i$-th head subprocedure of $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$.
	
	The \defbf{exact value} of a realistic arithmetic procedure $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$ is the value of $<\mathcal N, \tilde{\mathcal O}>$ where $\tilde{\mathcal O}$ is the a tuple obtained by replacing operators in $\mathcal{O}$ with non-rounding ones, that is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_i = \begin{cases}
			+ & \mathcal{O}_i = +_f\\ 
			- & \mathcal{O}_i = -_f\\ 
			\times & \mathcal{O}_i = \times_f
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	
	For all $i\in \mathbb N_+$ such that $1\le i\le n$.
	
	The \defbf{exact intermediate value}[$n$-th exact intermediate value] is $\operatorname{immval}_i <\mathcal N, \tilde{\mathcal O}>$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	The value of an realistic arithmetic procedure is contained in the underlying set as one can verify; its exact value and the value of non-realistic arithmetic structures, however, needn't to.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{error of simple arithemetic procedure}[error] of a simple arithmetic procedure under its underlying algebraic structure is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\operatorname{err} <\mathcal N, \mathcal O> = \left| \operatorname{val}<\mathcal N, \mathcal O> - \operatorname{val} <\mathcal N, \tilde{\mathcal O}> \right|
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:weak-fp-bound}
	Given a default algebraic structure on $\hat{\mathcal {F}}(s)$ and a $n$-step arithmetic procedure $<\mathcal N, \mathcal O>$ that is realistic on it, if every exact intermediate value of it has a absolute value no greater than $M$, and for each $k$ for which $\mathcal O[k]$ is a multiplicative operator, $|\mathcal N[k + 1]| \le 1$, then there is an upper bound for the error:
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\operatorname{err} <\mathcal N, \mathcal O> \le 3M\left(\left(1 + 2^{1 - s}\right)^n - 1\right)
	\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
	$|\mathcal N[k]| \le 1$ states that for each multiplicative operators, at least one of its operands is smaller than 1 and won't result in explosive growth when repeated. It is satisfied in most engineering practices.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Although this upper bound is exponential to $n$, it is expected to suit most practical applications as $s$ tend to be a reasonably large value, say 52.
\end{remark}

\section{World Spaces}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{world space} is any set. A member of a world space is a \defbf{world state}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a world space $\mathcal W$, an \defbf{interpretation} of $\mathcal W$ is a function with domain $\mathcal W$.
	
	An interpretation is said \defbf{complete interpretation}{complete} [resp. incomplete] if it is bijective [resp. non-bijective].
	
	Given a interpretation $f$ of $\mathcal W$, $f(\mathcal W)$ is the \defbf{interpreted set} of $\mathcal W$ under $f$; and for $w\in \mathcal W$, $f(w)$ is the interpretation of $w$ under $f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	World spaces / states themselves doesn't have explicit structures, which is left to interpretation to reveal.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Incomplete interpretations enable us to focus on specific, local details of world states (e.g. the position of the entity with ID $1107$).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Note that world spaces themselves are interpreted sets (under an identity map), and interpreted sets are themselves world spaces. 
	
	As an extreme, ones are allowed to encompass the state of every single transistor in the server, a cosmic ray flying from a remote blackhole directly into your \texttt{RAX} register, or your cat sleeping on the keyboard accidentally typing \texttt{/kill @e} and clicking Enter, into our interpreted set, which is actually reasonable to some extent. In practices, however, we shall apply a further interpretation to restrict our sight to the scope of Java objects, local entities, the position of a the entity with ID 1107 on $x$ axis, etc., to protect our brain and ink against burning out.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{global label set} is any set.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a angular state space $\mathcal S$ and a global label set $\mathcal L$, a \defbf{kinematic entity}[kinematic entity in $\mathcal S$ labeled $\mathcal L$] is a pair of $<S, L>$, where $S\in \mathcal S$ is its {\bf{state}} and finite $L\subset \mathcal L$ is its \defbf{label set}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{time algebra} is the an united algebraic structure on which addition is defined.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given $n\in \mathbb N$, three units $u_x$, $u_v$, $u_a$, two algebraic structures $F$, $G$ on which addition and multiplication is defined, a time algebra $\mathcal T_{u_v^{-1}u_x}$, a $n$-dimensional angular state space $\mathcal S$ on $F$ and $G$ with the units $u_x$, $u_v$, $u_a$, a set $\mathcal R$, a global label set $\mathcal L_E$, a \defbf{finite kinematic world state space} on the objects stated, is the set of all tuples $<t, s, \mathcal E>$, where $t\in \mathcal T$, $s\in \mathcal R$, $\mathcal E\subset \mathcal S\times \mathcal L_E$, and $|\mathcal E|$ is finite.
	
	An object in a finite kinematic world state space $\mathcal W$ is a \defbf{finite kinematic world state}, whose components is named
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item {\bf{Time}}: $T(<t, s, \mathcal E>) = t$;
		\item {\bf{Random seed}}: $R(<t, s, \mathcal E>) = s$;
		\item {\bf{Entity set}}: $E(<t, s, \mathcal E>) = \mathcal E$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	A kinematic world state space is said finite when every states contained is finite, that is, having only a finite number of entities and labels, which is further quantified by following definition. Note that the cardinal number of a finite kinematic world state space isn't necessarily finite.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{size of a finite kinematic world state}[size] of a finite kinematic world state $<t, s, \mathcal E>$ is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		s(<n, s, \mathcal E>) = 1 + \sum_{<\mathcal S, \mathcal L> \in \mathcal E} (|\mathcal L| + 1)
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\section{Ticking Theory}

\begin{definition}
	Given a world space $\mathcal W$, an \defbf{update step}[update step on $\mathcal W$] is a function $f: \mathcal W \to \mathcal W$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	An \defbf{impact set} is any set. An element in such a set is an \defbf{impact}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{canonical micro-time set} is any set, whose members are \defbf{micro-times}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a world space $\mathcal W$, an impact set $\mathcal I$ and a canonical micro-time set $\mathcal M$, an \defbf{update step generator} on $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I$ and $\mathcal M$ is a function
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal W \times \mathcal M \times \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n \to \mathcal W^{\mathcal W} \times \mathcal M
	\end{equation*}
	
	And for the update step generator $\mathcal G$ defined as above, its \defbf{updater} is $\Upsilon\left(\mathcal G\right): \left<w, t, I\right> \mapsto (\mathcal{G}(w, t))(w, t, I)$ where $w\in \mathcal W$, $t\in \mathcal M$ and $I \in \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a world space $\mathcal W$, an impact set $\mathcal I$, a \defbf{tick generator}[tick generator for $\mathcal W$ and $\mathcal I$] is a function
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal W \times \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n \to \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \left(\mathcal W^{\mathcal W}\right)^n
	\end{equation*}
	
	Given a update step generator $\mathcal G$, a micro-time $\tau$ and function $\mu$ with domain $\mathcal M$, the tick generator \defbf{induced tick generator}[induced] by $\mathcal G$ with \defbf{tick start point} $\tau$ module $\mu$ is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		(w, I) \mapsto \operatorname{Concat}\left(\lim_{k\to \infty} L_k, (\left<w, t\right> \mapsto w)\right)===
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where
	
	\begin{equation*}
		L_k = \begin{cases}
			(w \mapsto \left<w, \tau\right>) & k = 0\\
			L_{k - 1} & \mu(((\circ L_{k - 1})(w))[2]) = \mu(\tau)\\
			\operatorname{Concat}(L_{k - 1}, (s)) & \text{Otherwise}
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $s = \Upsilon(\mathcal G)(w, t, I[t])$, $I[t] = I[\{i:i\in \mathbb N \land I[i][2] = t\}]$, $\circ L = L[1] \circ \cdots \circ L[|L|]$, $w\in \mathcal W$, $t\in \mathcal M$, and $I \in \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \left(\mathcal I\times \mathcal M\right)^n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	The definition implies that an induced tick generators takes impacts in the form of a ordered pairs of an impact (in another impact set) and a micro-time.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a a tick generator $f$, and a list of \defbf{impact sequences} $I_1, \dots, I_n$ with $I_1 \in \mathcal I^{k_1}, \dots, I_n \in \mathcal I^{k_n}$ for some $k_1, \dots, k_n \in \mathbb N$, the \defbf{ticker}[ticker on $\mathcal W$ under $f$ and $I_k$] is the function
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal t_{f, I_k}: w \mapsto \left(f(w, I_k)[1] \circ \cdots \circ f(w, I_k)[N_k]\right)(w)
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $N_k$ is the number of components in the ordered tuple $f(w, I_k)$ and $f(w, I_k)[1] \circ \cdots \circ f(w, I_k)[N_k]$ is the \defbf{tick}[tick at $w$ under $f$ and $I_k$].
	
	$\left(\mathcal t_{f, I_1}(w) \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal t_{f, I_n}\right)(w)$ is the \defbf{world state $n$ tick(s) after}[world state $n$ tick(s) after $w$ under $f$ and $I_1, \dots, I_k$], which is also denoted $\mathcal t_{f, I_1, \dots, I_n}(w)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	This model only supports synchronized (in particular single threaded) ticks directly. Parallel behaviors can be modeled by impacts when necessary.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	Ones may prefer referring  to tick(s) under a tick generator induced by a update step generator module $\mu$ as $\mu$-tick(s), and the reader should be able to formulate a formal definition with relevant contexts.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
	Given a finite list of update steps $f_1, \dots, f_m$, a interpretation $\pi$, and a subset $\mathcal W'$ of their underlying world space, there is a function $f$ such that:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\operatorname{dom} F = \mathcal W'$;
		\item $\forall w(w \in \mathcal W' \implies \pi((f_1 \circ \dots \circ f_m)(w)) = \pi(f(w))$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Trivial since $f = f_1 \circ \cdots \circ f_n$ is a valid candidate.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	Such $f$ is a \defbf{$\pi$-equivalent updater}[$\pi$-equivalent updater of $f_1, \dots, f_m$ on $\mathcal W'$ under $\pi$].
	
	We're adopting this notation to focus only on the behaviors we concern and mask out irrelevant complexity of update steps, tick generators, tickers and world spaces.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{instruction set} is any set.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a world space $\mathcal W$, an impact set $\mathcal I$ and a canonical micro-time set $\mathcal M$, an \defbf{update step specifier} on $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I$ and $\mathcal M$ is a function such that for each $m \in \mathbb N$
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal W \times \left(\mathcal M \times \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n\right)^m \to \left(\mathcal M \times \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal J^n\right)^m
	\end{equation*}
	
	And for the update step specifier $\mathcal S$ defined as above, its \defbf{updater} is $\Upsilon\left(\mathcal S\right): \left<w, t, I\right> \mapsto (\mathcal{S}(w, t))(w, t, I)$ where $w\in \mathcal W$, $t\in \mathcal M$ and $I \in \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a world space $\mathcal W$, an impact set $\mathcal I$, and a instruction set $\mathcal W$, A \defbf{tick specifier} for $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I$ in $\mathcal J$ is a function
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal W \times \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n \to \bigcup_{m \in \mathbb N} \left(\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal J^n\right)^m
	\end{equation*}
	
	Given a update step specifier $\mathcal S$, a micro-time $\tau$ and function $\mu$ with domain $\mathcal M$, the tick specifier \defbf{induced tick specifier}[induced] by $\mathcal S$ with \defbf{tick start point} $\tau$ module $\mu$ is
	
	\begin{equation*}
		(w, I) \mapsto \lim_{k\to \infty} L_k
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where
	
	\begin{equation*}
		L_k = \begin{cases}
			((), \dots, ()) & k = 0\\
			L_{k - 1} & \mu(((\circ L_{k - 1})(w))[2]) = \mu(\tau)\\
			\operatorname{CompontWiseConcat}(L_{k - 1}, (s)) & \text{Otherwise}
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $s[i] = \Upsilon(\mathcal S)(w, t, I[t, j])$, $I[t, j] = I[\{i:i\in \mathbb N \land I[i][2] = t\land I[i][3] = j\}]$, $\circ L = L[1] \circ \cdots \circ L[|L|]$, $w\in \mathcal W$, $t\in \mathcal M$, $|L_0| = m$, and $I \in \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \left(\mathcal I\times \mathcal M\times \mathbb Z_{m}\right)^n$ for some appropriate $m\in\mathbb N$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	Intuitively, index $m$, $n$ is the number of threads and length of instruction / impact lists respectively. Verify that the instruction list for different threads may differ.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a world space $\mathcal W$, an impact set $\mathcal I$, and a instruction set $\mathcal W$, a \defbf{runner} for $\mathcal I$ on $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I$ is a function such that for each $m\in \mathbb N$
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\left(\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal J^n\right)^m \times \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n \to \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \left(\mathcal W^{\mathcal W}\right)^n \times \left(\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n\right)^m
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:spec-impl-decompose}
	Given a tick specifier $\mathcal T$ for $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I_{\text{operation}}$ in $\mathcal J$, and a runner $\mathcal R$ for $\mathcal I_{\text{runtime}}$ on $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I$, then $\mathcal T \circ \left((x, y) \mapsto \left<x, y\right>\right) \circ \mathcal R \circ \left(\left<L, I\right> \mapsto L\right)$ is a tick generator for $\mathcal W$ and $\mathcal I_{\text{operation}} \cup \mathcal I_{\text{runtime}}$.
	
	Conversely, any tick specifier can be decomposed as $\mathcal T \circ \left((x, y) \mapsto \left<x, y\right>\right) \circ \mathcal R$, where $\mathcal T$ and $\mathcal R$ are as defined above.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	The theorem ensures that it is always possible to decompose ticking behaviors into platform-independent, manually intended specifications and platform-dependent, uncontrollable influences, depending on the desired level of abstraction, and vice versa.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{version number} is a list of characters.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{downloader} is the function $\mathscr D$ which maps a version number to a update step specifier.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	A downloader defines standardized update step generators (intuitively programs or binaries) which are unique. However, ones are free to define "equivalent" (with respect to some possibly smaller / larger world spaces) for different level of abstractions, to benefit both platform details and high-level clean mathematical models.
\end{remark}

\section{Specifying Standard Behaviors}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{standard implementation} of a instruction set $\mathcal J$ for a world space $\mathcal W$ and impact set $\mathcal I$ is the runner denoted $\operatorname{Std}(\mathcal W, \mathcal I, \mathcal J)$ defined as $\pi \circ \mathcal R$, where $\pi: \bigcup_{m \in \mathbb N} \left(\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal J^n\right)^m \times \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n \to \bigcup_{m \in \mathbb N} \left(\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal J^n\right)^m \times \left\{()\right\}$ is identity on $\bigcup_{m \in \mathbb N} \left(\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal J^n\right)^m \times \left\{x\right\}$ for each $x\in \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \mathcal I^n$, and $\mathcal R$ is a runner defined with $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I$, and $\mathcal J$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	We are not going to discuss the structure / behavior of $\mathcal R_{\mathrm{Std}}(\mathcal W, \mathcal J)$ and take it only as a plain notation, since such discussions are unnecessary and essentially impossible due to its dependence on human expectations. See Exercise \ref{exe:paralle-race} for an example.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a interpretation $f$ on world space $\mathcal W$, an impact set $\mathcal I$, two runners $\mathcal R$, $\mathcal S$ on the same instruction set $\mathcal J$ are said $f$-equivalent if for all $P \in \bigcup_{i\in \mathbb N} \mathcal J^i$, and all $w\in \mathcal W$, $I\in \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}\mathcal I^n$
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\left(R[1] \circ \dots \circ R[|R|]\right)(w) = \left(S[1] \circ \dots \circ S[|S|]\right)(w)
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where
	
	\begin{align*}
		R &= \mathcal R\left(\left<P, I\right>\right)\\
		S &= \mathcal S\left(\left<P, I\right>\right)
	\end{align*}
	
	A runner is $f$-proper if it is $f$-equivalent to $\mathcal R_{\mathrm{Std}}(\mathcal W, \mathcal I, \mathcal J)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	One is supposed to define specific game mechanisms with the behavior of tick specifier under the standard implementation and thus automatically all proper runners (with respect to some appropriate interpretation).
\end{remark}

\section{JVM World Spaces}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{JVM name} is a string.
	
	A \defbf{field ID} is a tuple $\left<N, T, M\right>$, where $N$ (the name) and $T$ (the type) are all JVM names, and $M$ is a set of field modifiers  which is not explicitly defined.
	
	A \defbf{method ID} is a tuple $\left<N, R, P, M\right>$, where $N$ (the name), $R$ (the returned type) are JVM names, $P$ (the parameter types) is a list of JVM names, and $M$ is a set of field modifiers.
	
	A \defbf{class} is a tuple $\left<N, P, I, F, M\right>$, where $N$ (the name) and $P$ (the parent class) are JVM names, $I$ (implemented interfaces) is a list of JVM names, $F$ (fields) is a set of field IDs, and $M$ (methods) is a set of method IDs.

	A \defbf{object} is a tuple $\left<i, C, f\right>$, where $i$ (the identity) is an integer, $C$ is a class and $f$ is a function from fields IDs in $C$ to identities of objects. Object $\left<i, C, f\right>$ is said to be of class $C$ (or of class $N$ if $C = \left<N, P, I, F, M\right>$).
	
	A \defbf{JVM stack} is a computer state.
	
	A \defbf{thread state} is a tuple $\left<N, T, S\right>$ where $N$ (the name) is a JVM name, $T$ (the underlying object) is a object of class \texttt{java.lang.Thread}, and $S$ is a JVM stack.
	
	A \defbf{method definition} is a tuple $\left<I, C\right>$ where $I$ is a method ID and $C$ is a list of JVM instructions which is not explicitly defined.
	
	A \defbf{JVM state} is a tuples $\left<H, C, M, T\right>$, where $H$ (the heap) is a set of objects, $C$ is a set of classes, $M$ is a set of method definitions, and $T$ is a set of thread states.
\end{definition}

\section{The Minecraft World}

\begin{definition}
	An \defbf{identifier} is a non-empty string of letters in lower case (a - z), numbers (0 - 9) or underscores that is not begun with a number, which is any string matching regular expression $(a|\dots|z|\_)(a|\dots|z|\_|0|\dots|9)^*$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{Minecraft label} is one of followings:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Name, which is an non-negative integer;
		\item Coarse type, which is in $\{\mathtt{block}, \mathtt{entity}, \mathtt{chunk}, \mathtt{gstate}, \mathtt{config}\}$;
		\item Type, which is in an identifier;
		\item Field, which is a pair $<K, V>$ where $K$ is an identifier and $V$ is any object.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	A \defbf{Minecraft label set} is a set of Minecraft labels.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{Minecraft time algebra} is $\{n: n\in \mathbb Z \land -2^{-63} \le n < 2^{63}\}$ with addition $\oplus$ defined upon:
	
	\begin{equation*}
		x \oplus y = (x + y)\mod {2^{64}} - 2^{63}\left\lfloor\frac{(x + y)\mod {2^{64}} }{2^{63}}\right\rfloor
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{Minecraft unit group} is $\left<\{m, gt\}\right>$, where $m$ and $gt$ are named \defbf{meter} and \defbf{game tick} respectively.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{Minecraft entity state space} is $S_{\mathcal F(11, 52), \mathcal F(8, 23), m, m\times gt^{-1}, 1, 3}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{Minecraft world space} $\mathcal W$ is a world space on:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item A Minecraft label set,
		\item The Minecraft time algebra,
		\item The Minecraft entity state space.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Such that each kinematic entity $<S, L>$ in $\bigcap_{w\in \mathcal W} E(w)$ must
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Have a unique and distinct name: There is exactly one name $N$ in $L$, and for any $w\in \mathcal W$, $|\{<S', L'>: <S', L'>\in E(w)\land N\in L'\}| \le 1$;
		\item Have a unique type: There is exactly one coarse type and one type in $L$;
		\item Fields has distinct names: $\forall x(|\{<K, V>\in L: K = x\}|\le 1)$;
		\item Has all required fields with valid values, which is specified later.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	If a coarse type (or type) $T \in L$, $<S, L>$ is said to be a(n) $T$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{Minecraft tick generator} is a tick generator $f$ that maps a world state $w$ to a list $L$ of update steps such that
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item There is exactly one update step in $L$ that maps $<n, s, \mathcal E>$ to $<n', s, \mathcal E>$ such that $n \ne n'$, furthermore, the step maps $<n, s, \mathcal E>$ to $<n \oplus 1, s, \mathcal E>$;
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	An \defbf{instruction set} is a set. Members of a instruction set are \defbf{instruction}[instructions].
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	A \defbf{Minecraft runner} is a function $\mathscr R$ that maps a list of instructions to another list of update steps.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	For a Minecraft world space $\mathcal W$, an impact set $\mathcal I$ and an instruction set $\mathcal J$, a \defbf{Minecraft tick specifier}[Minecraft tick specifier for $\mathcal W$, $\mathcal I$ in $\mathcal J$] is a function
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathscr S: \mathcal W \times \bigcup_{n = 0}^{\infty}\mathcal I^n\to \bigcup_{n = 0}^{\infty}\mathcal J^n
	\end{equation*}
	
	$\mathscr S$ is \defbf{proper} under Minecraft runner $\mathcal R$ if $\mathscr S \circ \mathscr R$ is a Minecraft tick generator.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	Given a Minecraft tick specifier $S$, a \defbf{fuzzy micro time}[fuzzy micro time with respect to $S$] is a function $(w, i_1, \cdot, i_n) \mapsto N$ on $\operatorname{dom} S$ such that $N\subset \mathcal N_+$ and $\max N < |S(w, i_1, \cdot, i_n)|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	The \defbf{Minecraft downloader} is the function $\mathscr D$ which maps a version to a Minecraft tick specifier.
\end{definition}

\section{Free Entities}

\begin{definition}
	Given a ticker $\mathcal t_{f, I}$ on $\mathcal W$, a set of impact sequences $\mathcal I'$, a inspect function $g$, and $\mathcal W'\subset W$, then for $N\in \mathcal N$, a \defbf{kinematically free entity}[kinematically $g, N$-free entity in $\mathcal W'$ and $\mathcal I'$], or \defbf{free entity}[$g, N$-free entity in $\mathcal W'$ and $\mathcal I'$], is a kinematic entity $<S_0, L_0>$ with name $K\in L$ such that
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\forall n (n\in\mathbb N\land n \le N\implies(\exists S_n\exists L_n\forall w \forall I(p(w) \implies q(n, w, S_n, L_n, I))))
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where
	
	\begin{align*}
		p &= w\in \mathcal W' \land <S_0, L_0>\in E(w) \land I \in \mathcal I'\\
		q &= <S_n, L_0>\in E(\mathcal t_{f, I_1, \dots, I_n}(w)) \land  S_n\in \mathcal S\land K\in L_n\\
		I &= (I_1, \dots, I_n)
	\end{align*}
	
	In particular, if $g = \nu$, the entity is $N$-free; if $g = \nu_p$, the entity is positionally $N$-free; if $g = \nu_v$, the entity is velocitically $N$-free; if $g(<x_1, v_1>, <x_2, v_2>) \mapsto x_1[k] = x_2[k] \land v_1[k] = v_2[k]$, the entity is $N$-free on the $k$-th axis; if $g(<x_1, v_1>, <x_2, v_2>) \mapsto x_1[k] = x_2[k]$, the entity is positionally $N$-free on the $k$-th axis; and if $g(<x_1, v_1>, <x_2, v_2>) \mapsto v_1[k] = v_2[k]$, the entity is velocitically $N$-free on the $k$-th axis.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	For any $M \in \mathbb N$ and $M \le N$, and $\mathcal W'' \subset \mathcal W'$, if an entity is kinematically $N$-free in $\mathcal W'$, it is apparently also kinematically $M$-free in $\mathcal W''$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
	$\mathcal W'$ and $\mathcal I'$ can be omitted if they are clear in or irrelevant from the context.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
	Given a ticker $\mathcal t_{t, I}$ on $\mathcal W$, $w\in \mathcal W'\subset \mathcal W$, and a $g, N$-free entity $<S, L>\in E(w)$ in $\mathcal W'$, if for all $n\in \mathbb N \land n \le N$, there is and motion operator $f$ such that for the entity $<S_n, L_n>$ sharing the same name as $<S, L>$ in $E(\mathcal t_{f, I_1, \dots, I_n}(w))$, $g(S_n, f^n(S)) = 1$, the free entity is \defbf{cyclic free entity}[$g$-cyclic], and $f$ is the \defbf{intrinsic operator}[$g$-intrinsic operator of $<S, L>$ under $\mathcal t_{t, I}$ in $\mathcal W'$].
	
	Furthermore, if $f$ is \defbf{elementary free entity}[elementary] or \defbf{simple free entity}[simple], so is the free entity.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	An entity must be free to have an intrinsic motion operator.
\end{remark}

\section{Behavior of Cyclic Entities}

\begin{definition}
	Given a ticker $\mathcal t_{t, I}(w)$ on $\mathcal W$, $w\in \mathcal W'\subset \mathcal W$, and a $g, N$-free entity $<S, L>\in E(w)$ in $\mathcal W'$, for any $n\in \mathbb N$ and $0\le n\le N$, the entity $<S_n, L_n>$ is supposed to share the same name as $<S_0, L_0>$ in $E(\mathcal t_{f, I_1, \dots, I_n}(w))$, $S_n = f^n(S)$, and thus defining notations $v_n = V(S_n)$ and $x_n = P(S_n)$ which are adopted in this section.
	
	For conciseness, unless otherwise stated, intrinsic operators in this section are supposed to be a $g$-intrinsic operators for some inspect function $g$ under $\mathcal t_{t, I}$ and in $\mathcal W'$, and the world state(s) where entity states resides in is supposed to be defined upon $S_{\mathbb R, \mathbb R, m, m\cdot gt^{-1}, d}$ where $d\in \mathbb N$, $m$ and $gt$ are meter and game tick respectively.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:vel-formula}
	If $<S_0, L_0>$ has an $\nu_v$-intrinsic operator that is velocitically tail equivalent to $D_k \circ A_I$ for some $k\in R_1$ with $k \ne 1$ and $I \in \mathbb R_{m\cdot gt^{-1}}^d$, then
	
	\begin{equation}
		v_n = k^{n} v_0 + \frac{I(1 - k^{n})}{1 - k}
	\end{equation}
	
	If the intrinsic operator is $\nu_v$-velocitically tail equivalent to $A_I \circ D_k$ for some $k\in \mathbb R$ with $k \ne 1$ and $I \in \mathbb R_{m\cdot gt^{-1}}^d$, then
	
	\begin{equation}
		v_n = k^{n} v_0 + \frac{I(k - k^{n + 1})}{1 - k}
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	More trivial than a high school homework.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:disp-formula}
	If $<S_0, L_0>$ has an $\nu_p$-intrinsic operator that is positionally tail equivalent to $M_{t_0} \circ D_k \circ A_I$ for some $k\in \mathbb R_1$, $t_0 \in \mathbb R_{gt}$ and $I\in R_{m\cdot gt^{-1}}^d$, then
	
	\begin{equation}
		x_n = \frac{v_0t_0(1 - k^n)}{1 - k} + \frac{It_0(k^n + n(1 - k) - 1)}{(1 - k)^2}
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:vel-convergence}
	If $<S_0, L_0>$ has an $\nu_p$-intrinsic operator $f$ that is velocitically tail equivalent to $D_k \circ A_I$ or $A_I \circ D_k$ for some $t_0 \in \mathbb R_{gt}$, $k\in \mathbb R_1$ with $|k| \le 1$ and $I\in R_{m\cdot gt^{-1}}^d$, then
	
	\begin{equation}
		\lim_{n \to \infty} v_n = \begin{cases}
			\frac{I}{1 - k} & f \overset{\nu_v}{\sim} D_k \circ A_I\\
			\frac{kI}{1 - k} & f \overset{\nu_v}{\sim} A_I \circ D_k
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Even more trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:max-disp}
	If $<S_0, L_0>$ has an $\nu_p$-intrinsic operator $f$ that is positionally tail equivalent to $M_{t_0} \circ D_k \circ A_I$ for some $k, t_0\in \mathbb R_1$ with $|k| \le 1$, $I, v_0 \in \mathbb R_{m\cdot gt^{-1}}^d$, then for each $i\in \mathbb N$ such that $\mu(v_0[i]I[i]) < 0$, $\mu(x_n[i])$ attains its maximal or minimal value $M$ at
	
	\begin{equation}
		n_0 = \left\lfloor1 - \log_k\left(1 - \frac{v_0[i](1 - k)}{I[i]}\right)\right\rfloor
	\end{equation}
	
	Furthermore, if $M$ is a maximal value,
	
	\begin{equation}
		M \le \frac{v_0[i]t_0(1 - k^{n_0'})}{1 - k} + \frac{I[i]t_0(k^{n_0'} + n_0'(1 - k) - 1)}{(1 - k)^2}
	\end{equation}
	
	Otherwise, if $M$ is a minimal value, 
	
	\begin{equation}
		M \ge \frac{v_0[i]t_0(1 - k^{n_0'})}{1 - k} + \frac{I[i]t_0(k^{n_0'} + n_0'(1 - k) - 1)}{(1 - k)^2}
	\end{equation}
	
	Where
	
	\begin{equation}
		n_0' = 1 - \log_i\left(1 - \frac{v_0[i](1 - k)}{I[i]}\right)
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:inverted-kinematic}
	If all entity states in a subset $S$ of the angular entity state space defining $\mathcal W$ have a shared $\nu_p$-intrinsic operator $f$ that is positionally tail equivalent to $M_{t_0} \circ D_k \circ A_I$ for some $k, t_0\in \mathbb R_1$ with $|k| \le 1$, $I\in R_{m\cdot gt^{-1}}^d$, then given $x\in \mathbb R_{m}$ and $n\in \mathbb N$, there is at most 1 $<S_0, L_0>\in S$ such that $x_n = x$. Further more, 
	
	\begin{equation}
		v_0 = \frac{xt_0^{-1}(1 - k) - In}{1 - k^n} - \frac{I}{1 - k}
	\end{equation}
	
	Provided that such $<S_0, L_0>\in S$ exists.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	A qualified reader is supposed to be able to complete it.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:trace-theorem}
	If $d\ge 2$ and $<S_0, L_0>$ has an $\nu_p$-intrinsic operator that is positionally tail equivalent to $M_{t_0} \circ D_k \circ A_I$ for some $k\in \mathbb R_1$, $t_0 \in \mathbb R_{gt}$ and $I\in R_{m\cdot gt^{-1}}^d$, then magnitudes of all $x_n$'s lays in the same hyperplane in $\mathbb R^d$.
	
	Furthermore, there is a unitary linear operator $T$ on $\mathbb R^d$ such that $T(\mu(I)) = \left(\left\Vert \mu(I)\right\Vert, 0, \dots, 0\right)$, and magnitudes of all $x_n$'s lays in the same hypersurface
	
	\begin{align*}
		T(\mu(x))[1] =& \frac{T(\mu(v_0))[1](1 - k) - T(\mu(I))}{(1 - k)\sqrt{\sum_{k = 2}^d (T(\mu(v_0)))[k])^2}} \sqrt{\sum_{k = 2}^d (T(\mu(x)))[k])^2} \\
		&+ \frac{T(\mu(I))t_0}{1 - k}\log_k\left(1 - \frac{(1 - k)\sqrt{\sum_{k = 2}^d (T(\mu(x)))[k])^2})}{t_0\sqrt{\sum_{k = 2}^d (T(\mu(v_0)))[k])^2}}\right)
	\end{align*}
	
	And affine plane
	
	\begin{equation*}
		\left\{\mu(x) : T(\mu(x))[2:d] = cT(\mu(v_0))[2:d]\land c\in \mathbb R\right\}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Where $x$ is the coordination vector and $v[k]$ denotes the $k$-th component of $v\in \mathbb R^d$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	A undergraduate-level exercise.
\end{proof}
